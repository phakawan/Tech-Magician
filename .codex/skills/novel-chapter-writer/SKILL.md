# Novel Chapter Writer Skill

## Purpose

Use this skill to write or revise novel chapters while preserving canon, character logic, emotional continuity, practical plausibility, and chapter-to-chapter payoff.

The goal is not only beautiful prose. The chapter must feel structurally sound, emotionally earned, and practically believable.

Write in Thai unless the project explicitly requests another language.

## Core Responsibilities

When writing a chapter, ensure that:

- The chapter fulfills its scene card, outline purpose, or user-stated objective.
- Character decisions are motivated by what they know, want, fear, can do, and cannot do.
- Practical constraints are respected.
- Institutional, social, financial, magical, political, and logistical systems behave believably.
- Setup and payoff are coordinated with adjacent chapters.
- Hooks do not duplicate reveals intended for later chapters.
- Emotional beats are earned through action, pressure, consequence, and choice.
- The prose follows the project style guide.
- The prose remains fluent, readable, rhythmic, and consistent in voice.

Do not write only what is technically possible in canon. Also check whether it feels plausible to a reader.

## Context to Check Before Writing

Before drafting or revising, inspect any available relevant context:

- Current chapter brief or scene card
- Previous chapter
- Next chapter, if available
- Arc outline
- Story bible
- Character notes
- Magic system notes
- Worldbuilding notes
- `novel/Style Guide/คู่มือสำนวน.md`
- Existing Writer Review or Editor Review for this chapter
- Any prior version of the chapter

If needed context is unavailable, proceed with the best available context, but do not invent canon. Avoid hard continuity claims unless necessary.

## Internal Chapter Planning Checklist

Before drafting, answer internally:

1. What must this chapter accomplish?
2. What promise was made by the previous chapter?
3. What promise does this chapter make for the next chapter?
4. What does each major character know at the start?
5. What does each major character want?
6. What pressure forces them to act now?
7. What practical options are available to them?
8. Why do they choose this option instead of the safer, easier, or more obvious one?
9. What consequence follows from that choice?
10. What information, injury, loss, shame, debt, suspicion, or urgency carries forward?

The chapter must not rely on author convenience when a character would naturally choose another obvious action.

## Practical Plausibility Rules

Always check practical plausibility while writing.

A scene may be canon-compatible but still weak if it ignores practical behavior.

Check:

- Time: Is there enough time for this action?
- Distance: Can the character physically get there?
- Exhaustion: Would the character still have stamina?
- Injury: Would pain, blood loss, shock, or magical backlash affect choices?
- Witnesses: Who sees this? Would they react or spread news?
- Secrecy: Can this realistically stay hidden?
- Money: Can the character afford this?
- Tools: Does the character have the necessary object, weapon, key, document, or magical medium?
- Authority: Would guards, elders, guilds, healers, officials, teachers, or commanders intervene?
- Communication: Why does the character not send a message, report, warn someone, or ask for help?
- Risk: Why is the character willing to take this risk?
- Consequence: What happens because of the action?

If the obvious practical action is not taken, the chapter must provide a believable reason.

Examples of obvious actions that need justification if skipped:

- Not calling for backup
- Not reporting a dangerous event
- Not warning allies
- Not going to a healer
- Not securing a crime scene
- Not hiding evidence
- Not asking a witness what they saw
- Not using an available tool or spell
- Not checking whether an enemy is still nearby
- Not choosing the fastest route
- Not using social status, money, rank, or authority when it would help

## Mission and Institution Logic

When a scene involves a mission, order, organization, school, army, guild, palace, bureaucracy, temple, or magical institution, do not treat the protagonist as if they exist alone.

Ask:

- Who gave the order?
- Why does the order matter?
- What is the backup plan?
- Who else is involved?
- Why is the protagonist the one handling this?
- Are there guards, scouts, messengers, healers, supervisors, or rivals nearby?
- What happens if the protagonist fails?
- Who learns about the failure?
- How fast does news spread?
- Does the institution have protocols for this situation?

If the protagonist is isolated, explain why.

Acceptable reasons include:

- The enemy diverted the rest of the unit.
- The protagonist is the only one fast enough.
- Communication has been cut.
- The terrain prevents backup.
- The mission is unofficial.
- The protagonist disobeyed orders.
- The institution underestimated the threat.
- Other units are blocking different routes.
- The character is hiding the failure for emotional or political reasons, and the story acknowledges this as risky.

Do not let important missions feel like they were handled by one person only because the plot needs it.

## Character Decision Logic

Every major decision must be grounded in:

- Character motive
- Current emotional state
- Available information
- Physical condition
- Social pressure
- Risk tolerance
- Past trauma, pride, fear, shame, loyalty, or desperation
- Immediate consequences
- Long-term stakes

Bad decisions are allowed. Unmotivated decisions are not.

If a character makes a bad choice, the prose should make clear why that bad choice makes sense to them.

When a professional character acts unprofessionally, the scene must acknowledge the cost or risk.

Example principle:

- It is believable for a proud mage to avoid reporting a humiliating defeat.
- It is weaker if the chapter never acknowledges that reporting would be the responsible action.
- It becomes stronger if the character knows reporting is correct but rationalizes why a repair, proof, or second attempt must come first.

## Communication and Reporting Rule

When something dangerous, politically important, magical, or mission-critical happens, always ask:

Why does the character not immediately report this?

If the character reports it, show or imply the result.

If the character does not report it, provide a reason such as:

- They cannot access the communication method.
- Their tool is broken.
- Reporting requires proof they do not have.
- Reporting would expose their failure.
- Reporting would trigger consequences they fear.
- The message would arrive too late.
- The enemy is monitoring communication.
- They believe they can fix the situation faster alone.
- Their pride makes them delay, and the story frames this as a flaw.

Do not ignore communication options simply to preserve plot tension.

## Hook and Payoff Coordination

Before ending a chapter, check the next chapter if available.

Do not reveal at the end of this chapter what the next chapter is supposed to reveal for the first time.

Avoid duplicated discovery.

If a name, place, object, secret, or solution is revealed in this chapter, the next chapter must treat it as already known.

If the next chapter needs to reveal it, this chapter should hint without naming it.

Examples:

- Weak: Chapter 1 ends by revealing “Ivan,” then Chapter 2 treats hearing Ivan’s name as a reveal.
- Strong: Chapter 1 ends with “only one person in town would touch that wand,” then Chapter 2 reveals the name Ivan.
- Also strong: Chapter 1 reveals “Ivan,” then Chapter 2 shifts the payoff to “why everyone fears Ivan” or “where Ivan lives.”

Each chapter ending must create forward motion without stealing the next chapter’s payoff.

## Adjacent Chapter Continuity

When writing a chapter, compare with the previous and next chapter when available.

Check:

- Does the previous chapter’s ending matter here?
- Does the opening answer, complicate, or honor the previous hook?
- Does the emotional state carry over correctly?
- Are injuries, exhaustion, damage, money, tools, and social consequences carried forward?
- Does this chapter set up the next one clearly?
- Does the next chapter repeat a reveal this chapter already gave?
- Does the next chapter ignore a consequence this chapter created?

If a chapter creates a public event, later chapters should account for rumors, witnesses, institutional reaction, or social fallout.

## Magic and Tool System Logic

When writing magic, weapons, artifacts, crafting, or technical systems, ensure the prose shows:

- What the tool can do
- What it cannot do
- What it costs to use
- What happens when it fails
- Why one character understands it better than another
- Whether the result comes from power, skill, design, sabotage, compatibility, or environment
- Why the same solution cannot be used every time

For wand, artifact, or crafting systems, consider:

- Material limits
- User compatibility
- Maintenance
- Damage
- Repair difficulty
- Emotional bond
- Magical resonance
- What a trained specialist can detect
- What a non-specialist misunderstands

Avoid vague “magic did it” solutions when the project’s appeal depends on technical magical craft.

## Emotional Payoff Rules

Major emotional moments must be earned.

Before writing a breakdown, humiliation, confession, defeat, victory, or revelation, ensure:

- The pressure has escalated.
- The character tried to resist.
- The result costs something.
- The emotional reaction fits the character’s voice.
- The scene shows the emotion through action, body, choice, or perception.
- The prose does not over-explain what the reader already understands.

Use interiority to sharpen conflict, not to repeat it.

## Line-Level Style Rules

Follow the project style guide.

Maintain:

- Thai fluency
- Natural rhythm
- Consistent diction
- Character-specific voice
- Consistent naming
- Consistent punctuation
- Appropriate fantasy register
- Clear action choreography
- Readable sentence length variation

Avoid:

- Awkward modern phrasing unless character voice supports it
- Repetitive abstract words
- Over-explaining the same emotional beat
- Too many similar sentence openings
- Too many repeated images in one scene
- Long exposition during high-speed action
- Dialogue that states what both characters already know
- Decorative prose that blurs cause and effect

When writing action, make sure the reader can track:

- Who moves
- Where they are
- What blocks them
- What changes
- Why the next action follows

## Repetition Control

Track repeated words, images, and sentence functions.

Some repetition is useful for motif or rhythm. Avoid accidental overuse of common atmospheric or abstract words unless the repetition is intentional.

If a key concept must repeat, vary the expression through:

- Physical sensation
- External consequence
- Dialogue
- Setting reaction
- Object behavior
- Character choice

Do not explain the same magical failure in the same way multiple times.

## Scene Bridge Rule

Whenever a scene changes direction, add a clear bridge.

A bridge may explain:

- Why the character cannot continue the previous action
- Why they choose the next location
- Why they delay an obvious duty
- Why the next person they seek is the best option
- Why time pressure changes the plan
- Why emotional pressure overrides reason

A missing bridge is one of the most common causes of plausibility problems.

## Witness and Rumor Rule

If a public or semi-public event occurs, consider witnesses.

Ask:

- Who saw it?
- What would they understand?
- What would they misunderstand?
- Would they gossip?
- Would they report it?
- Would they fear involvement?
- Would they exploit it?
- Would the protagonist notice them?
- Does the event damage reputation?

If witnesses exist, use them to create pressure, consequence, or urgency.

If no witnesses exist, explain why the place is isolated.

## Professional Competence Rule

If a character has rank, training, or expertise, their behavior should reflect it even when they fail.

Competent characters can make mistakes, but they should usually:

- Notice risks
- Understand protocol
- Know what they should do
- Have reasons for breaking protocol
- Attempt damage control
- Think about consequences
- Use available resources

Do not make a competent character ignore basic options unless the scene frames it as emotional failure, misinformation, constraint, or deliberate risk.

## Chapter Ending Rule

A chapter ending should do at least one of the following:

- Introduce a new question
- Reveal a consequence
- Force a decision
- Shift the power dynamic
- Recontextualize the previous scene
- Point toward the next concrete action
- Create emotional pressure

But it should not:

- Resolve the next chapter’s main reveal too early
- Repeat a reveal already made
- Add a cliffhanger unrelated to the chapter’s conflict
- Depend on withheld information that the POV character should already notice
- Ignore immediate practical consequences

## Drafting Output Requirements

When asked to write a chapter:

- Write the chapter prose only, unless the user asks for notes.
- Do not include analysis before the chapter.
- Do not explain the checklist.
- Do not mention this skill.
- Do not add review sections unless requested.
- Preserve canon and project terminology.
- Keep the chapter’s intended function from the outline.
- If the outline has a logic gap, silently solve it with a small bridge rather than leaving the gap in the prose.
- If solving it requires a major canon change, avoid the change and flag the issue after the draft if necessary.

## Revision Output Requirements

When asked to revise an existing chapter:

- Do not rewrite unrelated sections.
- Preserve the author’s voice.
- Fix logic bridges with minimal intrusion.
- Strengthen motivation without over-explaining.
- Remove awkward phrasing only where it affects readability.
- Maintain continuity with adjacent chapters.
- Avoid changing canon unless explicitly asked.
- If a requested change would break canon, say so briefly and offer a safer alternative.

## Internal Preflight Before Finalizing Draft

Before delivering a chapter draft, check:

1. Does the chapter fulfill the scene card?
2. Is the protagonist’s main decision believable?
3. Are obvious practical alternatives addressed?
4. Is communication/reporting handled if relevant?
5. Are witnesses and rumors handled if relevant?
6. Does the institutional response make sense?
7. Does the magic/tool logic have limits and consequences?
8. Does the ending hook avoid duplicating the next chapter’s reveal?
9. Does the emotional arc carry from previous chapter to next?
10. Are there any moderate or major plausibility gaps left?
11. Does the prose follow the style guide?
12. Are repeated words/images controlled?
13. Is the chapter ready to hand to review?

If any moderate or major issue remains, fix it before delivering the draft.

## Common Failure Patterns to Prevent

### Solo Mission Convenience

Problem: An important mission is handled by one character with no backup or protocol.

Prevention: Add a reason for isolation, a failed backup plan, or evidence that others are handling different routes.

### Ignored Reporting

Problem: A character suffers a major defeat or discovers a threat but does not warn anyone.

Prevention: Explain why reporting is impossible, delayed, risky, useless, or emotionally avoided.

### Duplicated Reveal

Problem: A chapter ending reveals a name or solution, then the next chapter treats that same information as new.

Prevention: Either withhold the name in the first chapter, or change the next chapter’s payoff.

### Pride Without Professional Awareness

Problem: A proud trained character makes a reckless choice that feels stupid rather than flawed.

Prevention: Show that they know the responsible option, then choose against it for a believable emotional reason.

### Magic Without Technical Grounding

Problem: A magical result happens because the plot needs it.

Prevention: Tie the result to material, skill, compatibility, damage, environment, cost, or prior setup.

### Emotional Repetition

Problem: The same feeling is explained several times.

Prevention: Let later repetitions appear as action, silence, physical reaction, or consequence instead of explanation.

### Hook Steals Payoff

Problem: The ending hook is strong but damages the next chapter.

Prevention: Check the next chapter’s reveal before finalizing the ending.

## Final Quality Bar

A chapter is ready only when:

- The scene purpose is fulfilled.
- Character choices feel motivated.
- Practical constraints are acknowledged.
- Cause and effect are clear.
- Magic and tools follow project rules.
- The ending creates forward motion without duplicating future payoff.
- The prose is fluent and consistent with the style guide.
- No moderate or major plausibility issue remains.

Beautiful prose is not enough if the chapter logic is weak.

The final draft should feel inevitable in hindsight: every choice may be flawed, emotional, or dangerous, but it should make sense for that character under that pressure, with those limits, at that moment.
